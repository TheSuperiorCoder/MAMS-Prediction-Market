How MAMS Prediction Market works

A prediction market turns a question about the future into a price. The price is the school's best guess at how likely it is. Everything here uses play money, but the ideas are the same ones real prediction markets use.

Prices are probabilities

Every share pays Ⓜ1 if it's right and Ⓜ0 if it's wrong. So a “Yes” share priced at 30¢ means the market thinks Yes is about 30% likely.

The idea behind it is old: lots of people each know a little, and putting money on the line is a good way to pull that knowledge together. If you know something the market doesn't, you can profit by buying, and your buying moves the price toward the truth. People who are often wrong lose Bucks and have less influence over time. Talk is free; trades aren't.

Example. “Yes” is at 30¢. You spend Ⓜ30 and get about 100 shares. If Yes happens, they pay Ⓜ100 (a Ⓜ70 profit). If not, they're worth Ⓜ0. Buying pushes the price up a little, so your average price is a bit above 30¢.

Who is on the other side?

Real markets like Polymarket match buyers with sellers in an order book. That works with millions of traders, but with a school's worth of people there would often be nobody to trade with. Here an automated market maker (called LMSR) always offers a price. Buying an outcome pushes its price up, and selling pushes it down.

Every market is set up so that no single person can push it around too easily. On a 50/50 yes/no market:

  • a Ⓜ100 bet moves the price to about 55%
  • a Ⓜ500 bet moves it to about 70%
  • going all in with Ⓜ1,000 moves it to about 82%

So a price only gets close to 0% or 100% when lots of people agree.

You can sell shares back before betting closes, at the current price.

Over / Under markets

Some markets are about a number, like “What will the physics class average be?” with a line of 85%. Buy Over if you think the real number will be above the line, or Under if below. When the number is announced, the winning side's shares pay Ⓜ1 each. If it lands exactly on the line, nobody wins and everyone is refunded.

Making markets (and earning from them)

  • Proposing a market is free, though you must bet at least Ⓜ{{ CREATOR_MIN_BET|int }} on your own market when you propose it. It's held until an admin approves the market, then placed as a normal bet. If the proposal is rejected or withdrawn, it comes back.
  • There are no per-trader entry fees. Instead, when the market resolves, any money left over in the pot after paying Ⓜ1 per winning share goes to the market creator (capped at Ⓜ1,000).

Where winnings come from

Every bet goes into the market's pot. When the market resolves, each winning share is paid Ⓜ1 from the pot, so the losers' money pays the winners. If there's money left over, up to Ⓜ1,000 goes to the market creator. If the pot comes up short, the site covers the difference.

Late trading fee

Trading right before betting closes, when the answer may already be obvious, costs a fee. It's charged on buys and sells and paid to the house:

fee = {{ '%g'|format(LATE_FEE_RATE) }} × shares traded × P × (1 − P) × T

P is the average price per share in your trade (so 55¢ means P = 0.55). P × (1 − P) is largest at 50¢ and small near 0¢ or 100¢.

Because that would make near-certain outcomes cheap to grab at the last minute, buys pay whichever is larger: the fee above, or

fee = {{ '%g'|format(LATE_SNIPE_RATE) }} × shares bought × P × P × T

This only matters for expensive shares (above about 80¢). Sells only pay the first fee, so cashing out stays cheap.

Loans

Players can lend each other {{ CURRENCY }} on the Loans page. The lender picks the amount, a flat interest rate and how many days the borrower has to pay it back, and can offer it to one player or to anyone. The money is held until someone borrows it. Borrow Ⓜ100 at 10% and you owe Ⓜ110, which you can pay back early, in parts or all at once. A loan that goes past its due date gets a {{ (LOAN_LATE_FEE * 100)|round|int }}% late fee (paid to the lender), and anything still owed is taken from the borrower's cash automatically, including money they receive later, until the loan is paid off. Loans count towards net worth: money you're owed adds to yours, and money you owe is subtracted.

There are limits so nobody can borrow and vanish. You can owe at most the larger of Ⓜ500 or half of your cash plus the sell-now value of your bets, minus your debts, across at most {{ LOAN_MAX_ACTIVE }} loans, and you can't lend while you're borrowing. While a loan is overdue you can't buy, lend or create markets (selling and repaying still work), and everything you receive goes to your lenders. If it's still unpaid {{ LOAN_GRACE_DAYS }} days after the due date, the lender can force a sale of your bets in open markets or take them over at their current sell-now value, only as much as covers what you owe. Bets in markets that have closed are paid to lenders when the market resolves. Debts never expire, and admins can extend or forgive a loan.

Everyone also has a credit score from 300 to 850, worked out from how you've handled loans: paying back on time (recent and larger loans count most), how much of your limit you're using, how long you've been borrowing, how many different lenders you've repaid, how steady your balance is, and how many loans you've taken lately. A loan that's overdue or in default caps your score. The score sets how much you can borrow, and lenders can require a minimum score or browse borrowers by score. See yours, and what's moving it, on your credit report.

Read the rules, not just the title

A market called “Will the bill pass?” might really mean “signed into law by December 31” or “passed by the House”. Many arguments come from people betting on the title without reading the rules. Every market has a Rules box with:

  • Resolves YES if…: exactly what has to happen.
  • Source: where the answer will be published.
  • Otherwise: what happens in edge cases, like a postponement or a tie.

The written rules are locked once anyone has bet, so nobody can change the deal after the fact. Admins can still add a dated clarification.

Yes/No and multiple choice

Most markets are Yes/No. Some ask “who” or “which”, with several choices. In those, only one choice can be right, so all the prices always add up to 100%. Buying one choice makes the others a little cheaper.

How results are decided

  1. Betting closes at the time shown on the market.
  2. An admin proposes a result once it's known.
  3. For {{ window_hours }} hours, anyone who traded in that market can dispute it and say why.
  4. If nobody disputes, the result becomes final and winning shares are paid automatically.
  5. If someone disputes, payment pauses until an admin reviews it. They either confirm the result or change it, which starts a new {{ window_hours }}-hour window.

The idea is the same as Polymarket's “optimistic” resolution: assume the proposed answer is right unless someone speaks up. Most results go through without any argument.

Where it can go wrong

  • Prices aren't perfect. In a small market, one big bettor can push the price around. Long shots also tend to trade a bit too high, because cheap lottery tickets are tempting.
  • Insider information. Rewarding people who know more is the whole point, but it also rewards people who know something they shouldn't, like a result they already heard. Trades by admins and market creators are labelled so everyone can see them, and admins can block someone from a market. If you already know the answer, don't bet.
  • Admins decide in the end. They propose results and settle disputes, so if a result looks wrong, use the dispute button.
